The first light

About 380,000 years after the Big Bang, the universe cooled enough for electrons and nuclei to combine into neutral atoms. Light could finally travel freely. We observe this ancient afterglow today as the cosmic microwave background.

05 / CELESTIAL MECHANICS

How positions are calculated

ORBIT solves Kepler’s equation using JPL’s fitted planetary elements for 1800–2050. The model is date-driven and useful for learning, but it is not a replacement for high-precision Horizons ephemerides.

HOW TO USE THIS LIBRARY

Start with the NASA and IAU references for definitions and primary mission context. Distinguish measured values from models, artistic visualizations and educational approximations. ORBIT is an interactive learning experience, not a live ephemeris or scientific data archive. ORBIT offers original procedural soundtrack compositions and interface sonifications; these are creative synthesizer designs, not recordings of planets. Sound cannot propagate through the vacuum of space.

OBSERVATORY GUIDE

Explore at your own pace.

ORBIT is an educational visualization. Planet positions use NASA/JPL approximate Keplerian elements fitted for 1800–2050, in the J2000 ecliptic frame. The Earth entry is the Earth–Moon barycentre proxy; the simulation does not apply light-time corrections. Scene distances are compressed and planet sizes exaggerated. Use JPL Horizons for precision work.

  • Drag to rotate your viewpoint; scroll or pinch to zoom.
  • Select a planet in the scene or use the navigation strip.
  • Set the ephemeris date, then advance time at the selected simulation rate.
  • Adjust simulation speed, pause orbital motion, or hide orbit guides.
  • Use Reset to return to the full-system view.
